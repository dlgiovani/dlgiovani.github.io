Some AI-written texts are absurdly easy to spot. Constructions like “X isn't just Y — it's Z”, lined up with the other red flags, convince the sharper reader that this sequence of words is not the result of flesh-and-bone fingers on a keyboard.

That human capacity to sense the writing style of an advanced linguistic system makes us wonder whether an LLM really could replace a writer. Language is far more than morphemes and phrases; it is subjective and so strangely complex, because it relates directly to feelings, sensations, uncertain memories and passions that a machine still comes nowhere near simulating.

Or, at least, that is what we think.

Certain AIs can already produce videos so realistic that they fool the overwhelming majority of internet users. It is not hard to imagine that text might be at that level too. The writer of this article, for instance, thinks he will never manage to convince you, reader, that this text was written the “old-fashioned way”. But he hopes you will take his word that it was. Because it was.

As if that weren't enough, there is another fact to talk about: what about the AI models that have not yet been released to the public?

Any number of models can be trained and refined by private or even governmental organisations, behind closed doors. Several technologies do in fact operate that way. We are facing a technology that can manipulate reality with fake digital media that is extremely well made and ever harder to tell apart from the real thing.

Today, right now, you can download onto a phone an AI that turns any photo into a video, customised with whatever the user asks for. And the result, of course, while not perfect, is sometimes good enough to convince someone that it really happened. All of that for free.

In short, perhaps the Dead Internet is not something being born, but rather something that has already grown and developed to the point where we can no longer tell what is alive and what is the result of cold arithmetic on a silicon interface.
